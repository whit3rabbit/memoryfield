## Findings

### What the numbers say

- All four baselines comfortably exceed **P@3 ≥ 0.8** on both domains. The hard target from the plan (PLAN.md §1: 'most lookups should be answerable at stub stage') is met or beaten by every baseline except dense (0.67–0.69).
- **FTS outperforms dense on these queries.** FTS hits P@3 = 0.97 on codebase and 0.98 on papers. Dense hits 0.95 and 0.91. This is the opposite of the conventional wisdom that 'dense wins retrieval' — and it's because the queries are short natural-language questions whose vocabulary overlaps strongly with the pages' text. Dense would presumably pull ahead on paraphrased queries (which we didn't include).
- **Hybrid does not beat FTS alone.** P@3 and R@5 are equal or within noise. MRR is slightly lower for hybrid (0.897 vs 0.928 on code, 0.877 vs 0.923 on papers). This is the most actionable finding: the RRF fusion is *not* justified by the data on these corpora. Either (a) FTS is doing all the real work and dense is diluting its ranks via RRF, or (b) our hybrid implementation needs different weights / k.
- **Stub-end rate is 0.67–0.82.** That means roughly two-thirds of lookups *could* end at the stub stage without the agent reading the body. This is the single most important number for the design — it justifies the whole 'stub-first' architecture. But it also means ~25% of queries genuinely need the body to answer.

### Where the design bets hold

1. **Pages-as-embedding-units, not chunks, work.** All baselines hit ≥ 0.88 P@3 on whole pages, so the chunking/extraction pipeline Cal argues against really isn't needed here.
2. **Stub summaries carry most of the answer.** A 67–82% stub-end rate means the 'summary is the answer' convention from PLAN.md §5 is delivering. Writes that don't follow that convention will pay real costs at lookup time.
3. **FTS5 + bm25 is a strong default.** It beats dense, is trivial to ship (in-process sqlite), and has no model dependency. For M1 this means the hybrid design should default to **FTS-first, dense as supplement** rather than the symmetric RRF we're running.

### Where the data surprised us

- **Dense does not beat FTS** on either domain. The plan's asymmetric design (FTS as workhorse, dense for paraphrased recall) is right; the symmetric RRF we implemented is wrong.
- **Queries are too 'easy'.** The query set was constructed by someone who knew the corpus vocabulary, so lexical overlap is high. M1 needs at least a paraphrased-query subset before we trust these rankings as production-typical.
- **Stub-end rate is domain-dependent.** Code (0.74 avg) lower than papers (0.79 avg). Code pages have longer bodies with concrete commands that agents often need; paper pages' summaries tend to be self-contained. The writing conventions in PLAN.md §5 may need different rules per domain.

## What M0 did not measure

- **Index size, indexing cost, embedding cost.** M0 builds the FTS index in-memory per query session; the real cost in M1 will be one-time per page edit and depends on the embedder choice.
- **Per-query latency.** The harness runs all queries serially; wall-clock numbers are dominated by Python startup, not retrieval.
- **Real embedder quality.** `dense` here is TF-IDF, not a real embedding model. We expect `nomic-embed-text-v1.5` or `bge-m3` (per PLAN.md §4) to change the numbers, especially on paraphrased queries we haven't built.
- **Paraphrased-query recall.** All 214 queries were authored with the corpus in front of the author. Lexical overlap is artificially high. M1 needs a paraphrased subset before we trust the P@3 ranking as production-typical.
- **Stub quality on real agents.** Stub-end rate is computed from `stub_sufficient: bool` labels set by the corpus author. Real agents may differ. M2 needs a human eval.
